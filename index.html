What happens at each type of front

A closer look at how each front forms, the weather it brings, what you would notice as it passes, and what it means for flying. The card for the scenario you have selected is highlighted.

Cold front

Cold air displaces warm air
How it works
Dense, cold air advances and wedges under the warmer air ahead of it, shoving the warm air upward along a steep boundary (roughly 1:50 to 1:100). Cold fronts are usually the fastest-moving fronts, often 20 to 35 knots, so the lifting is abrupt.
Weather
Towering cumulus and cumulonimbus build right along the boundary, producing a narrow band of heavy showers, thunderstorms, gusty winds, and sometimes squall lines or hail. Behind the front, skies clear quickly.
At the station
Temperature and dew point drop, pressure falls ahead of the front and then rises sharply, and the wind shifts clockwise (typically southwest to northwest). Visibility is poor in the rain, then improves fast.
Aviation impact
Turbulence, wind shear, and microbursts near thunderstorms, low ceilings in heavy precipitation, and possible icing aloft. The worst is usually short-lived, but gusty winds and bumpy, unstable air can linger behind the front.

Warm front

Warm air overrides cold air
How it works
Advancing warm air is lighter than the cold air it meets, so it cannot push it aside. Instead it glides up and over the cold air along a very gentle slope (roughly 1:150 to 1:300). Warm fronts move slowly, typically 10 to 25 knots, and their effects reach far ahead of the surface line.
Weather
Clouds thicken in sequence ahead of the front: cirrus, cirrostratus, altostratus, then nimbostratus with steady, widespread rain or snow, and low stratus or fog near the ground. Precipitation can last many hours over hundreds of miles. If the warm air is unstable, thunderstorms can hide inside the cloud deck.
At the station
Temperature and dew point rise, pressure falls steadily and then levels off, and the wind shifts clockwise (often southeast to south or southwest). Afterward the air turns warm, humid, and hazy.
Aviation impact
Prolonged IFR conditions with low ceilings and poor visibility, icing in cloud above the freezing level, and freezing rain or ice pellets where rain falls through sub-freezing air. Low-level wind shear is common just ahead of the surface front.

Occluded front

A cold front overtakes a warm front
How it works
A cold front moves faster than the warm front ahead of it and eventually catches up, squeezing the warm air between them and lifting it completely off the ground. This marks the mature-to-dissipating stage of a mid-latitude low. In a cold-type occlusion, the air behind the cold front is colder than the air ahead of the warm front and undercuts it. In a warm-type occlusion, the air behind is milder and rides up over the cooler air ahead.
Weather
A blend of warm and cold front weather. Thick layered cloud and steady precipitation spread ahead of the occlusion, while heavier showers and thunderstorms can develop where the warm air is forced high. The worst weather is often near the occlusion itself, and it fades as the lifted warm air cools and dries out.
At the station
A stretch of steady rain gives way to a clockwise wind shift and rising pressure as the cold air arrives. The temperature change is often modest because the warm air is already aloft.
Aviation impact
Widespread low ceilings and reduced visibility, icing, and turbulence across a broad area, with embedded thunderstorms possible. Because the warm air is aloft, weather can persist even after the surface boundary has passed.

Stationary front

Neither air mass advances
How it works
Cold and warm air press against each other, but neither is strong enough to displace the other, so the boundary barely moves (generally under about 5 knots). Winds on either side tend to blow roughly parallel to the front, in opposite directions.
Weather
Warm air gradually rides up over the cold air, much like a weak warm front, producing layered cloud, drizzle or steady rain, and fog along and on the cold side of the boundary. Pockets of stronger lift can trigger thunderstorms. Because nothing sweeps the front away, the same weather can sit over one area for days, raising the risk of flooding.
At the station
Little changes while the front hangs nearby, with temperature, wind, and pressure staying fairly steady. Eventually the front may weaken and fade, or a wave can form along it and turn it into a moving cold or warm front.
Aviation impact
Persistent IFR over a wide area, icing in cloud, and embedded convection. Pay close attention to the front's exact position, since a small shift can put an airport on either side of the cloud and rain.

Descriptions assume the Northern Hemisphere. Speeds, slopes, and wind shifts are typical values and vary with season and the strength of the system.
